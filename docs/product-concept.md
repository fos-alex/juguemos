# Ludi — Product Concept

*Chosen name. Trademark, domain, and app store availability still to be confirmed.*

**Tagline:** The play coach that knows your family by heart.

**First market:** Argentina (Buenos Aires first) · Later: Latin America

**Version 1:** Argentina only · ages 1–5 · responsive web

**Version:** 0.7 · September 2026 · Owner: Alex Otero

---

## 1. Vision

Ludi helps parents turn the time they have into play that is fun and meaningful. It knows who is in the family, what each kid loves, which toys are in the house, the weather outside, and what each child is working on learning. It uses all of that to answer one everyday question: *"¿Qué hacemos ahora?"* ("What should we do right now?")

Setting it up is as easy as sending a voice note about your family. Beyond play, it gives parents timely, practical tips for the stage their family is in, from the terrible twos to getting two kids to bed at once to preparing a toddler for a new sibling.

Over time it becomes a record of the family's shared moments and of the progress each kid has made.

## 2. Name

**Ludi** is Latin for "games", the plural of *ludus*, and the root of the Spanish *lúdico*, playful. It is short, it names what the product is about rather than a tool, and it is as easy to say in English as in Spanish if the product expands beyond the region. The app lives at `ludi.ar`.

The invitation stays in the app: Home's button says *¡Juguemos!*, "let's play," which was the product's name before Ludi.

Tagline options:

- *The play coach that knows your family by heart.* (current favorite)
- *Your family's next great idea.*
- *Play today. Grow every day.*

Spanish taglines still need to be written natively rather than translated, since this is the language most users will see first.

Alternatives considered: Nidito, Kindling, Playbook, and Tiny Tribe.

Trademark (INPI in Argentina), domain, and app store availability still need to be checked before the name is final.

## 3. First market: Buenos Aires, Argentina

Version 1 focuses only on Argentina, designed first around Buenos Aires (CABA and the greater metro area, AMBA). Other Latin American countries come later. Designing for one country and one city first lets the product feel local instead of translated.

### 3.1 Language and tone

The interface, activities, stories, and tips are written in Rioplatense Spanish, using *vos*, and local vocabulary such as *chicos*, *plaza*, *pileta*, *merienda*, and *jardín* (preschool). English is the second language at launch, both as an optional interface language and as the most requested language-learning goal. Portuguese becomes relevant later for Brazil.

### 3.2 Calendar and seasons

Seasons are reversed from the northern-hemisphere content most apps are built on. The product needs to follow the local calendar, including:

- Summer break (December to February), with heat, water play, and *colonias de vacaciones*.
- Winter break (July), a two-week peak for indoor plans and city programming.
- Día del Niño / Día de las Infancias in August, which is usually a long weekend packed with family events.
- Long weekends (*fines de semana largos*), Carnaval, and a summer Christmas.
- Special days with their own traditions, such as Día del Padre, Día de la Madre, Día del Amigo, and Día del Maestro, which are natural moments for themed activities (see 8.6).

### 3.3 Weather

Buenos Aires summers are hot and humid, with heat waves and sudden storms. Winters are cold and damp. Rainy-day and heat-wave modes matter more here than snow.

### 3.4 Home context

Many Buenos Aires families live in apartments with a balcony rather than a backyard. Activities need a "small space" tag, and the plaza is the default outdoor space since nearly every neighborhood has one.

### 3.5 Places and events

Family outings are plentiful: plazas and parks, free city programming, museums, and seasonal events. Today, parents discover these mostly through blogs, lifestyle media, and Instagram accounts rather than a dedicated app. That is an opportunity, but also a data challenge. Places like parks and museums are well covered by maps providers; time-limited events are not.

### 3.6 Culture

Traditional games (*la escondida*, *rayuela*, *la ronda*), the strong local tradition of children's music and literature, family Sundays, and the central role of *abuelos* should shape the content. Grandparents can be added as family members who join activities, even though they won't have their own accounts at first.

## 4. The problem

Parents want to spend quality time with their kids, but they hit four walls. The first is decision fatigue: at 6pm on a rainy Tuesday, nobody has ideas left. The second is that generic ideas don't fit. Lists online ignore the child's age, interests, the toys already at home, the apartment they live in, and the time available. The third is that learning is scattered. Parents have goals like "count to ten," "ride a bike," or "speak more English," but no simple way to practice consistently or see progress. The fourth is that advice is overwhelming. Parenting information is everywhere, but little of it is timely, practical, and specific to their kids' ages and situation.

## 5. Market landscape (September 2026)

Each piece of the product exists somewhere, but nothing combines them around a family's goals over time, and very little of it is built for Argentina.

**Developmental activity libraries (Kinedu, Lovevery).** These have large, expert-backed catalogs for ages 0–6. Kinedu is Spanish-language and has a strong presence in Latin America, which makes it the closest regional competitor. Both are static and stage-based, and some parents find them repetitive. Lovevery ties its app to its own toy subscription.

**Activity generators (Unplug Journey, Kindred AI, generic chatbots).** These take age, time, weather, and budget and return ideas. They are stateless: they don't remember the family, its toys, or its goals, and they are mostly English-first.

**Local activity discovery.** Apps like Hoop and Yuggler focus on the US and UK. In Buenos Aires, family-plan discovery happens through blogs, lifestyle media, and Instagram.

**Story apps.** This category splits into three models, and it's mostly English-first:

- *AI generators* (Oscar Stories, Bedtimestory.ai, and dozens more) let parents pick a character, setting, or moral, then produce an illustrated and often narrated story. The phone does the storytelling.
- *Sleep audio* (Moshi) is built around the transition to sleep. Its stories slow in rhythm as they progress, the narration gets sleepier, and the background sounds fade toward the end.
- *Parent read-aloud* (Readmio) is closest to what Ludi wants. The parent reads a story from the phone while the app listens and plays sound effects. The stories intentionally have no illustrations so the child's attention stays on the parent, and the app offers storytelling tips such as character voices and dramatization cues. Its stories come from a fixed library rather than being personalized.

Stories alone are not a product. Ludi combines the parent-read model with personalization: stories in Rioplatense Spanish, starring the family's kids, toys, and pet, adapted to the time of day, with performance notes for the parent.

**Where Ludi is different:** persistent family memory (people, pets, interests, toys) plus goal-driven play that adapts, timely stage-based tips, and a moments journal, all designed locally for Argentine families.

## 6. Target users

The primary users are the parents in a Buenos Aires household with one to three children aged 1–5, who want screen-free quality time and care about their children's development. Both parents use the app, each with their own style. Many will be interested in English as a second language for their kids.

Version 1 covers ages 1–5 so the content can go deep for the early years; older ages come later. Families expecting a new baby are a key secondary segment, since that is a moment of high motivation and real need.

The founder's own family is the first test household: Alex, Caro, their two-year-old Milán, and Inca, the family dog. They are used as the example family throughout this document.

## 7. The core loop

The whole product is built around one repeating cycle:

**Know the family → suggest an activity or tip → play → capture the moment → learn from it → suggest better next time.**

Every feature either feeds this loop with better context (profile, toy box, weather, goals) or gets value out of it (moments, recaps, stories, tips).

## 8. Features

### 8.1 Conversational onboarding

The main way to set up Ludi is to talk to it. The parent sends a voice note (or types) describing the family in their own words: *"Somos Alex y Caro, tenemos a Milán, de dos años, y a Inca, nuestra mascota. A Milán le encantan los dinosaurios y los caballos, y tiene un tren de madera que no suelta."*

The agent pulls out who is in the family, ages, interests, toys, home type, neighborhood, languages, and upcoming changes. It then shows a summary card to confirm ("¿Está bien así?") and asks at most one or two follow-up questions for essentials that are missing. Everything else can be filled in later. Nothing is saved to the profile without the parent's confirmation.

The same channel keeps the profile current over time. A parent can send "Milán ya dejó el chupete" or "compramos una pileta inflable," and the app updates goals and the toy box accordingly.

A traditional step-by-step onboarding form is also available for parents who prefer it or can't send audio, and it doubles as the place to review and edit the profile. The conversational path is the default and should be the one most families use.

The target is onboarding in under two minutes, with the first activity suggestion shown immediately afterward. This depends on accurate transcription of Rioplatense Spanish, including unusual children's names. As a privacy default, audio should be discarded once it has been transcribed.

### 8.2 Family and household

**Accounts.** One parent signs up, creates the family, and invites their partner by link or email. Both adults have their own login and share the same family: kids, pets, toy box, goals, tips, stories, and moments. When the invited partner joins, they get a short conversational onboarding about themselves only, since the family is already set up.

**Members without accounts.** Kids, pets, and grandparents are family members who take part in activities but don't log in.

**Each parent's play style.** Every adult has their own profile, because two parents rarely play the same way. It records:

- Energy level, from chill to energetic.
- The kinds of play they enjoy, such as crafts, sports, reading, music, outdoors, or cooking.
- What they'd rather avoid, such as messy crafts or loud games.
- The languages they speak with the kids. This supports "one parent, one language" households, where the English-speaking parent receives the English practice activities.

The "who's playing" input on the Play screen includes which adults are taking part, and suggestions adapt. On the same rainy afternoon, the energetic parent might get a living-room obstacle course while the calmer parent gets a cooking and storytelling activity. When both play together, suggestions blend their styles. Check-ins and feedback are learned per adult, so the app gets better at suggesting for each parent over time.

**Households.** The data model separates the family (the people) from the household (the place: location, home type, and toy box). Version 1 supports one household per family, but the model allows more later, such as separated parents with two homes, the grandparents' house on Sundays, or a summer house, each with its own location and toys.

**Daily rhythm.** The profile records each child's usual routine: wake-up time, naps, meals, and bedtime. This can come up naturally in onboarding ("Milán se duerme a las nueve") and is refined over time. Play and Story Time use it to match the energy of suggestions to the moment, so a high-energy game doesn't show up twenty minutes before bed.

**Upcoming changes and dates.** The profile stores upcoming changes, such as a baby on the way with an approximate due date or a child starting *jardín*, which unlock goals and tips (see 8.7 and 8.8). It also stores family dates like birthdays and anniversaries, which feed special-day activities (see 8.6).

### 8.3 Toy box

The app keeps a remembered inventory of what the family has at home, and it refers to each toy the way the family does. A two-year-old doesn't know "a plastic dinosaur figure." They know *el dinosaurio chiquito*. When the app, a story, or a parent reading an activity aloud uses that name, the child recognizes it right away.

#### Two layers for every toy

Every toy has two parts:

- **Family name.** The label the family uses at home, such as *el dinosaurio chiquito*, *el tren grandote*, *el osito marrón*, or *el caballo percherón*. This is the only name that appears in suggestions, stories, reminders, and moments. A toy can also have aliases, like the child's own way of saying it ("el tuto"), so voice input still matches it.
- **Description for the AI.** A plain description of what the toy actually is, so the AI can reason about how to use it: type, size, material, notable features, and what it's good for. Size matters for safety, since it tells the AI whether a toy is safe for a younger sibling or can be hidden, stacked, or taken in the bath.

For example:

| Family name | Description for the AI | Tags |
|---|---|---|
| *El dinosaurio chiquito* | Small plastic T-rex, about 8 cm, fits in a toddler's hand, hard plastic, not safe in the bath | Pretend, hiding games, counting · Milán · Favorite |
| *El tren grandote* | Wooden train set with 4 magnetic cars and about 20 track pieces | Building, fine motor, sequencing · Shared |
| *El osito marrón* | Soft brown teddy bear, about 30 cm, washable | Pretend, caring, calm-down, bedtime · Milán · Favorite |
| *El caballo percherón* | Plastic draft horse figure, about 15 cm, from a farm animal set | Pretend, animal sounds, farm play · Milán |

#### Names are relative, not literal

Family names describe how the child tells toys apart, not what the toys actually are. If a child has two horses, the bigger one might be *el caballo grande* even though it's only 15 cm tall, simply because the other one is smaller. *El tren grandote* may just be the bigger of two trains.

This has three consequences for the product:

- **The AI never infers facts from the family name.** Size, material, and safety always come from the description, not from words like *grande* or *chiquito* in the name. A toy called *el caballo grande* can still be small enough to be a choking hazard for a baby sibling.
- **Toys that are told apart are linked.** When toys are named in relation to each other, like *el caballo grande* and *el caballo chico*, the app records them as a related pair or set. This allows activities like "¿Dónde está el caballo chico? ¡Se escondió del grande!", which also practice concepts like big and small, more and less, and comparing.
- **Similar toys prompt a distinguishing name.** When a parent adds a toy that looks like one already in the box, the app asks how the child tells them apart ("¿Este es el grande o el chico?") instead of guessing.

The app never corrects or "fixes" a family name, even when it doesn't match the toy's real size or type.

Each toy also records which child it belongs to or suits, whether it's a favorite, and what it's good for (building, pretend play, fine motor, counting). The family name and the description are always kept separate, so the AI understands the toy while the family only ever hears its own words for it.

#### Adding toys

Voice is the most natural way to add toys, because parents already use the family names out loud: *"Tiene el dinosaurio chiquito, que es un T-rex de plástico, y el tren grandote de madera."* The agent separates what the family calls each toy from what it is, and asks only when something is unclear ("¿Cómo le dicen al osito?").

Writing works the same way, as free text or through a simple form with a name field and an optional description.

Adding toys from a photo is planned for a later version, not v1. When it arrives, the app will identify the toy and draft the description itself, then ask the parent what the family calls it.

Parents can also pick from common household materials such as cardboard boxes, pots and spoons, blankets, and chalk. These don't need family names, although they can have them.

#### How the toy box is used

The toy box feeds every other feature, always using family names. Activities prefer things already at home ("Escondé *el dinosaurio chiquito* debajo de un almohadón y buscalo con Milán"). Stories cast the toys as characters (*el caballo percherón* goes on an adventure with *el osito marrón*). Goals are practiced with familiar objects, and moments can be tagged with the toys that took part.

A light touch keeps it current. Names can change as kids grow, and parents can rename a toy at any time. When a toy stops appearing in logged activities, the app can occasionally ask *"¿Siguen teniendo el tren grandote?"*

### 8.4 Play

This is the main screen, built around one big button: **"¿Qué hacemos ahora?"**

#### One tap, one game

The parent shouldn't have to say anything. Tapping the button immediately shows one game, using smart defaults:

- **Time:** 30 minutes.
- **Who's playing:** the kids at home and the parent holding the phone.
- **Context the app already knows:** current weather, time of day and the kids' routine, home type, the toy box, each parent's play style, active goals, upcoming special days, and recent activities, so it avoids repeats.

The app shows one game at a time, not a list, to keep the decision fast and the time on screen short. The defaults are visible and can be changed with a tap or by voice ("tenemos una hora", "algo tranqui", "estamos en la plaza"), but changing them is never required.

#### Another idea, then refine

Tapping the button again shows a different game. Each skip is a signal the app learns from, especially when skips follow a pattern (always skipping messy crafts, for example).

After three skips, the app offers a quick refinement instead of a fourth random idea: *"¿Te ayudo a encontrar algo?"* It shows a few one-tap chips, such as more or less time, calmer or more energetic, inside or outside, and the activity categories, plus the option to just say what they're in the mood for. The next suggestions use the refinement, and the app can remember it if it reveals a lasting preference.

#### What a suggestion shows

Each suggestion includes a short title, why it fits ("usa *el tren grandote*, practica contar, ideal para una tarde de lluvia"), materials needed, simple steps, and ways to make it easier or harder. When there is more than one child, suggestions explain how each child participates at their own level.

#### Activity categories

| Category | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Move** | Active, high-energy play: obstacle courses, dancing, ball games, balcony-friendly versions |
| **Create** | Arts, crafts, building, drawing, music-making |
| **Pretend** | Role play, imaginative scenarios, puppet shows, using the toy box |
| **Explore** | Outdoors and nature: the plaza, parks, bugs, leaves, water, sensory play |
| **Learn** | Activities tied to an active goal (see Grow) |
| **Low-energy** | For tired parents or wind-down time: the parent can sit or lie down, calm games, quiet sensory play, pre-bedtime |
| **Helpers** | Kitchen and household tasks turned into play: preparing the *merienda*, sorting laundry, watering plants |
| **Out & About** | Nearby places and outings: plazas, museums, libraries, free city events |

An activity can belong to more than one category. Low-energy is a first-class option, not an afterthought, because it reflects how parents actually feel at the end of the day.

### 8.5 Nearby and weather

The app is weather-aware, suggesting indoor alternatives when it rains and shade or water play during a heat wave. It also suggests nearby places (plazas, parks, kid-friendly spots) through a maps provider, filtered by distance and suitability for the kids' ages. Seasonal peaks such as winter break and Día del Niño get special attention.

### 8.6 Special days and holidays

The app knows the calendar of the family's country, plus the family's own dates, and proposes themed activities in the days leading up to each one.

**Dates it knows about.** In Argentina, this includes Día del Padre (June), Día de la Madre (October), Día del Amigo (July 20), Día del Niño (August), Día del Maestro (September 11), and Día de la Primavera (September 21). It also covers Easter, Carnaval, Christmas, Reyes, and national holidays and long weekends, including patriotic dates like May 25 and July 9, with age-appropriate activities such as making an *escarapela*. From the profile, it adds each member's birthday, the parents' anniversary, the pet's birthday, and the new baby's due date.

**Themed activities.** These include gift-making (the classic "let's build a present for papá"), cards and drawings, decorations, a special breakfast, a surprise plan, and stories themed around the day. Suggestions scale with the time left. Two weeks out, the app can propose a gift project spread over several short sessions. Three days out, it suggests something quick.

**Surprise mode.** When an activity is a gift for another adult in the household, the app plans it with one parent and hides it from the other parent's suggestions, reminders, and moments until the day. For example, ten days before Día del Padre, Caro gets a gift project to make with Milán for Alex, and none of it appears on Alex's phone. After the celebration, the moment appears in the shared journal.

**Per-country calendars.** Ludi maintains its own Latin American holiday calendar, with one calendar per country, starting with Argentina for v1. Dates differ across the region. Mother's Day, for example, is in October in Argentina but in May in many other countries. Argentina also moves some holidays each year. The holiday calendar therefore has to be a maintained data source per country and per year, not something hardcoded.

### 8.7 Grow (goals)

Goals turn play into progress.

**Proposed goals.** The app suggests goals based on each child's age, interests, and what's happening in the family. The parent can accept them, edit them, or write their own. Bilingual goals (English at launch) are always among the suggestions.

| Age band | Example proposed goals |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | First English words, naming animals and their sounds, stacking blocks, pointing to body parts |
| 2–3 | Colors and shapes, counting to 5 or 10, songs in English, jumping with both feet, early self-dressing |
| 3–5 | Letters and sounds, counting to 20, simple addition, balance bike, everyday phrases in English, taking turns |
| 5–8 (after v1) | Reading simple books, addition and subtraction, riding a bike, telling time, short conversations in English |

Interests shape how a goal is practiced. For a dinosaur-loving child, counting can become "count the dinosaurs' footprints."

**Life transition goals.** Some goals come from what's happening in the family rather than from skills. They are proposed when the profile shows a change coming, and they run on a timeline. Examples include starting *jardín*, leaving diapers, giving up the pacifier, moving house, and the flagship example below.

**Getting ready for a new sibling.** When the family adds a baby on the way, the app proposes a goal to prepare each older child, adapted to their age and to the time left before the due date. Before the birth, activities include pretend-caring for a doll or stuffed animal from the toy box (feeding, rocking, gentle touch), looking at the child's own baby photos together, helping prepare the baby's space, making a gift for the new sibling, and role-playing the day of the birth: who will stay with them and what will happen. Personalized stories cast the child as the big brother or sister, featuring their own toys and interests.

The goal continues after the birth, when adjustment is hardest. Activities shift to "big sibling helper" jobs, games the older child can play near the baby, naming feelings, and short daily one-on-one time with a parent.

**Practice.** Each goal gets a rotating set of short activities, many using the toy box, that show up in Play under Learn.

**Check-ins.** After an activity, the parent taps one quick rating: "nailed it," "getting there," or "not today." This drives adaptation, so the app offers more practice where it's needed and moves on when a skill is mastered.

**Recaps.** Once a month by default (parents can change the frequency), the app sums up what was practiced and achieved, celebrates wins, and gently flags skills that need attention ("You haven't practiced colors in two weeks. Here's a 10-minute idea.").

### 8.8 Parenting tips

A section of short, practical tips matched to the family's ages, composition, interests, and current situation. Tips are meant to be read in about a minute and used the same day.

**Stage-based tips.** These follow each child's age. With Milán at two, for example, Alex and Caro would get tips on the terrible twos: handling tantrums, getting through the "no" phase, offering limited choices, and setting calm limits. Other stages bring tips on sleep routines, starting *jardín*, sharing, and screen time.

**Multiple-kids tips.** When the profile has more than one child, the app adds tips for managing them together. Examples include getting two kids to bed at the same time, feeding them together at different ages and stages, handling sibling fights, making one-on-one time for each child, and finding activities that work for a toddler and an older sibling at once.

**Situation-based tips.** These are triggered by what's happening, such as a baby on the way, the first weeks with a newborn and an older child, winter break with the kids at home all day, or a move.

**Connected to play.** Where possible, a tip links to an activity ("Try this calm-down game tonight") or a goal, so advice turns into action.

**Interaction.** Parents can save tips, mark them as helpful or not (which improves future suggestions), and ask the agent follow-up questions grounded in the tips library.

**Content standards.** Tips are evidence-based and reviewed by professionals such as pediatricians and child psychologists, drawing on sources like the Sociedad Argentina de Pediatría. They are written in a warm, non-judgmental tone. Tips are not medical advice: health or development concerns always point parents to their pediatrician, and the app never diagnoses.

### 8.9 Story Time

A **"Hora del cuento"** button creates a personalized story for the parent to read aloud to the kids. The phone never does the reading. The parent's voice, face, and presence are the point.

#### Choosing a story

Tapping the button shows **three plot options**, each a title and a one-line teaser. They are built from what the app knows: the kids, their toys by family name, Inca, their interests, active goals (a counting story, English words, getting ready for a sibling), upcoming special days, and the time of day. For example:

- *El caballo grande pierde su herradura*: Milán and *el caballo percherón* search the house for it.
- *Inca y el dinosaurio chiquito van a la plaza*: an adventure in the neighborhood.
- *El osito marrón no tiene sueño*: a gentle story for bedtime.

The parent picks one, asks for three new options, or describes their own idea by voice ("un cuento donde Inca encuentra el tren grandote").

#### Time-aware stories

The story's energy follows the time of day and the child's routine, not a fixed clock.

**Morning and afternoon stories** can be energy-packed: adventures, silly moments, repetition kids can shout along with, and invitations to move, like roaring, galloping, or jumping.

**Bedtime stories** (roughly in the hour before the child's usual bedtime) are calm and slow. The plot has no scary moments or cliffhangers, uses soft sensory images and gentle repetition, and slows down toward the end. Like Moshi's sleep stories, the arc is designed around the transition to sleep. The preferred ending has the main character going to sleep, although that is a preference rather than a hard rule; any calm, settled resolution works.

The parent can always override the mood ("uno con pilas" or "uno bien tranqui").

**Length** also adapts, based on the child's age and the moment. For a two-year-old, the default is a short story of a few minutes. The estimated reading time is shown before starting.

#### Notes for the parent

The story includes performance notes for the parent: small cues set apart visually from the story text, so the parent knows not to read them aloud. Types of notes include:

- **Voices:** "Voz grave y lenta para *el caballo grande*."
- **Volume and pace:** "Susurrá esta parte." "Levantá la voz: ¡sorpresa!"
- **Sounds the parent makes:** "Hacé el ruido del tren: ¡chu-chú!"
- **Gestures and actions:** "Hacé galopar los dedos por el brazo de Milán." "Tocale la nariz."
- **Props from the toy box:** "Si tenés *el osito marrón* a mano, hacelo hablar."
- **Pauses and questions:** "Pausá y dejá que Milán diga 'guau'." "¿Qué ruido hace Inca?"

The pauses and questions come from dialogic reading, a well-researched approach to reading aloud where the parent invites the child to take part. Prompts are matched to age. For ages 1–2, the app uses fill-in-the-blank pauses ("El perro hace…"). For ages 2–4, it uses simple who/what/where questions. For ages 4–5, it connects the story to the child's own life ("¿Te acordás cuando fuimos a la plaza?").

At bedtime, the notes change character. There are fewer questions, since a tired child wants to listen rather than be quizzed, and more calming cues: slow down, lower your voice, yawn, dim the light, stroke their back, and take a slow breath together.

Parents who don't want the notes can turn them off.

#### The reading screen

The screen is designed for reading aloud in a dim room with a child on your lap. It uses large, easy text and stays on while reading. Near bedtime it switches automatically to a warm, dark night mode. The story has no illustrations, so the child's attention stays on the parent rather than the phone, which follows Readmio's approach.

#### After the story

Toddlers love hearing the same story again, so every story can be saved to the family library and reread with one tap. Favorites can get sequels with the same characters. A quick one-tap reaction (loved it / not really) improves future stories. At bedtime, the app never asks for anything after the story ends. Saving a moment can wait until the next day.

### 8.10 The agent (games and conversation)

A conversational companion that knows the family. It can:

- Create family read-alouds, simple rhymes, and interactive "choose what happens next" tales beyond Story Time.
- Adjust length, tone, reading level, and language, including bilingual stories with English words mixed in.
- Suggest or invent games, including board games the family owns (from the toy box), traditional Argentine games, and new games made from materials at home.
- Answer everyday "what should we do?" requests and parenting follow-up questions conversationally.

Stories and games can be saved to the family library and linked to moments.

### 8.11 Moments journal

This is the family's shared memory. After any activity, the parent can add a photo, a short note, and who took part; the activity, goal, and date are attached automatically. Moments form a timeline, a milestone record ("First time riding without training wheels," "Milán's first word in English: *dog*, pointing at Inca"), and material for recaps.

Future ideas: a year-in-review summary and a printed keepsake book.

## 9. Content: how activities are made

Activities are the heart of the product, and they're created in a loop that combines inspiration, AI, and human judgment.

**Inspiration.** The team researches existing play ideas online, in books, and from parents to understand what works and what kids love. These sources inspire original activities; the goal is never to copy someone else's content.

**Creation by agents.** AI agents draft new activities in Rioplatense Spanish and tag them with the full taxonomy (age, time, energy, category, materials, skills, safety, and so on).

**Human review.** Every activity is reviewed by a person before it enters the catalog, checking that it's fun, safe, age-appropriate, and clearly written. The review focuses especially on safety constraints, since these are what the AI may never change later.

**Room to tailor.** Each activity is written as a flexible template rather than a finished script. It has a fixed core that has been reviewed: the idea, the steps that matter, the age range, and the safety rules. It also has open slots that the agent fills in for each family at the moment of the suggestion:

- Which toys to use, by family name.
- Characters, themes, and interests (dinosaurs for one family, horses for another).
- The pet, the siblings, and which parent is playing.
- Language (for example, English words for a bilingual goal).
- Difficulty, duration, and energy level within the reviewed range.

For example, a reviewed "hide and find" activity for ages 1–3 becomes, for Milán, *"Escondé el dinosaurio chiquito debajo de un almohadón y buscalo con Milán. Cuando lo encuentre, ¡que ruja!"* The agent tailors everything inside the slots but never goes outside the reviewed core, such as suggesting a toy that's too small for the child's age.

The same approach applies to goals and parenting tips, which are also reviewed by professionals before release. Stories are generated fresh each time, but within reviewed guidelines for age, tone, and bedtime content.

## 10. Product principles

All product decisions are guided by **The Ludi Constitution** (separate document), which sets out our five commitments, the guardrails that protect them, and how to resolve conflicts between them. The principles below show how it applies to this concept.

**Local first.** Built for Buenos Aires families: their language, calendar, weather, homes, and places. Expansion adapts content per country rather than translating it.

**Screen-light by design.** The app helps plan and remember; the play itself happens off-screen.

**Safety first.** The foundation is a curated, tagged activity catalog. AI personalizes, adapts, and explains, but doesn't invent unsafe activities from scratch. Every activity carries age-safety tags such as choking hazards, supervision level, and heat or water warnings.

**Trusted advice.** Tips are professionally reviewed, practical, and never replace the pediatrician.

**Private by default.** Child data (names, ages, photos, location, family events like a pregnancy) is treated with the highest care. Collect only what's needed, never sell or share it, and comply with Argentina's Ley 25.326 (overseen by the AAIP) first, then with each new market's rules.

**Warm, not preachy.** Encourage without guilt. "Not today" is a valid answer.

**Bilingual from day one.** Rioplatense Spanish first, English as both an interface option and a core learning goal.

## 11. Out of scope for now

Payments, subscriptions, and token pricing; countries other than Argentina; children older than five; native mobile apps (v1 is responsive web); adding toys from a photo; curated event listings beyond maps data; accounts for caregivers beyond the parents, such as grandparents or nannies; more than one household per family; a WhatsApp or chat-channel MVP; and social or community features.

## 12. Decisions and open questions

### Decisions for v1

| Topic | Decision |
|---|---|
| Market | Argentina only, designed first around Buenos Aires. |
| Ages | 1–5. Older ages come later. |
| Platform | Responsive web, usable one-handed on a phone, including voice through the browser microphone. Native apps come later. |
| Toy box | Voice and text only. Photo identification comes in a later version. |
| Content | Activities are inspired by research, drafted by AI agents, and reviewed by humans. Each one is a template with a reviewed core and room for the agent to tailor it to each family (see section 9). |
| Places and events | Maps data is enough for v1. |
| Recaps | Monthly by default; parents can change the frequency. |
| Voice onboarding | Transcription is the default path. The technical challenge of accuracy for Rioplatense Spanish, children's names, and background noise will be solved during the build. |
| Holiday calendar | Ludi builds and maintains its own Latin American calendar, one per country, starting with Argentina. |
| Households | One household per family in v1. |

### Still open

- **Reviewers:** which Argentine professionals (educators, pediatricians, child psychologists, occupational therapists) will review activities, goals, and tips?
- **Catalog size:** how many activities, goals, and tips are needed before launch?
- **Name:** is Ludi available as a trademark (INPI), and what are the native Spanish taglines? The domain is `ludi.ar`.
- **Interface language:** should English be available as an interface language in v1, or only as a learning goal?
- **Expansion:** after Argentina, which country comes next: Uruguay (same dialect and culture) or larger markets like Mexico and Colombia?

## 13. How to start planning

The goal of planning is to validate the core loop with real Buenos Aires families before building everything. The steps below run in order and each one feeds the next.

Write the jobs** | Write 8–10 concrete scenarios from real family life ("Rainy Saturday, 30 min, toddler is cranky, I'm tired"; "Baby due in two months, how do I prepare my 3-year-old?"; "Two kids, one bedtime"; "Día del Padre is in 10 days"). ### Suggested MVP scope

The first release should include conversational onboarding (with the form as a fallback), a shared family with partner invite and per-parent play styles, the family profile (including upcoming changes like a new baby), special days and holidays with themed activities and surprise mode, the toy box with family names and descriptions (voice and text), Play with categories and weather, nearby places from maps data, monthly recaps, Grow with proposed goals, the new-sibling goal, and check-ins, a first set of parenting tips (stage-based and multiple kids), the one-tap Play button with refinement after three skips, Story Time with three plot options, time-aware stories, and parent performance notes, and the moments journal.

After v1, likely next steps include photo identification for the toy box, curated Buenos Aires events, native mobile apps, ages above five, more than one household per family, and the first country beyond Argentina.
